# Survey Methodology
**Agent:** Agent 4 — Survey Designer
**Date:** 2026-06-10

---

## Design Rationale

Both surveys (`survey-group-a.md` for ages 6–12 and `survey-group-b.md` for ages 13–17) follow the same six-section structure so that results can be broadly compared across age groups while still being delivered in age-appropriate language.

**Section 1 — General Roblox Usage.** This section opens both surveys because it is low-stakes, concrete, and familiar. Asking children to describe how often and how long they play, what kinds of games they enjoy, and who they play with builds comfort and rapport before any questions about rules or restrictions are introduced. It also provides baseline usage context that helps interpret later responses (e.g., a child who plays daily with online-only contacts may have different exposure to rule-related information than one who plays occasionally with real-life friends).

**Section 2 — Awareness of Age Restrictions.** This section establishes what respondents already know about Roblox's age-related rules before asking for opinions or experiences. It is purely factual/awareness-based ("did you know," "have you seen"), which keeps it neutral and non-threatening. Group B receives an additional question (Q8) asking how they first learned about the rules, since older respondents can reliably recall and categorise information sources — a level of meta-reflection not expected of younger children.

**Section 3 — Perceived Effectiveness of Restrictions.** This section shifts from facts to opinions and feelings. For Group A, this is delivered as a simple emoji-based Likert scale ("Do the rules keep you safe?", "Do the rules make sense?") because young children can express degrees of feeling more reliably through faces than through abstract agreement scales. Group B receives the standard 5-point agreement Likert scale, which is appropriate for older respondents capable of evaluating institutional policy ("the age restrictions effectively protect younger users..."). Group B also receives an additional question (Q12) on whether Roblox enforces its rules adequately — a more evaluative/critical question suited to the greater abstraction capacity of teenagers.

**Bypass definition (inserted before Section 4/5 in both surveys).** Before any question referencing "bypassing" or "getting around the rules," both surveys insert the standard neutral definition (in age-appropriate language) verbatim from the skill. This ensures every respondent has a shared, neutral understanding of the term before being asked about it, and explicitly reassures respondents that they do not need to have done any of these things themselves — reducing anxiety and the risk of the question being read as an accusation.

**Section 4 — Bypass Awareness.** A single, low-stakes yes/no/not-sure question asking whether the respondent has ever *heard of* someone else bypassing restrictions. This is framed entirely in third-person/observational terms (a friend, classmate, sibling) and never asks about the respondent's own behaviour.

**Section 5 — Bypass Method Awareness.** This section is explicitly conditional: it is shown only to respondents who answered "Yes" to the Section 4 question. It is introduced with a reassuring framing statement reiterating that the questions are about awareness only. Each of the five defined bypass methods (parent/sibling account, false date of birth, VPN, multiple accounts, third-party/modified clients) is presented as its own standalone yes/no/not-sure item, plus an open "other methods" item with an optional free-text field. Presenting one method per question (rather than a combined checklist) avoids implying that these are a menu of techniques to consider, and keeps each judgement independent and easy to answer.

**Section 6 — Open-Ended Closing Question.** Both surveys end with a single optional open-text question giving respondents space to add anything not otherwise captured. This is framed warmly, explicitly marked optional, and reassures the respondent that "there are no wrong answers."

**Language calibration.** Group A language uses short sentences, concrete examples, and familiar vocabulary ("a grown-up's account," "a different birthday," "a special computer tool to make Roblox think they're in a different country"), and pairs the abstract Likert scale with emoji faces. Group B language uses fuller sentences, more precise/technical vocabulary (e.g., "VPN," "false date of birth," "modified versions of Roblox"), and the standard 5-point agreement scale, appropriate to a more abstract reasoning level.

## Question Type Justification

- **Yes/No (with "I'm not sure" option) — used throughout both surveys for awareness checks (Sections 2, 4, 5).** This format is simple, fast, low-burden, and minimises the chance of a child feeling "tested." Adding "I'm not sure" / "I can't remember" as a third option avoids forcing a binary answer when genuine uncertainty exists, which is especially important for younger respondents and for sensitive topics like bypassing.

- **Multiple choice (capped at the skill's specified limits — max 4 options for Group A Likert items, up to 6 for Group B selection items) — used in Section 1 (usage frequency, duration, game types, play companions) and Section 2 Q8 (Group B only, information sources).** Multiple choice keeps response burden low and produces structured, comparable data across respondents. "Select all that apply" is used where multiple genuine answers are plausible (game types, information sources), with an "Other: ___" free-text option to capture unanticipated responses without forcing a forced choice.

- **Likert scale — used in Section 3 (perceived effectiveness/safety/sense-making of the rules).** Likert scales are the standard tool for measuring degrees of agreement or feeling, which is exactly what Section 3 needs (how safe/sensible the rules feel). Group A uses a 5-point emoji-anchored scale (😊🙂😐🙁😞) because young children process facial expressions of feeling more intuitively than abstract verbal agreement labels; Group B uses the standard verbal 5-point scale (Strongly agree → Strongly disagree), which is well understood by teenagers and widely used in adolescent research.

- **Short answer / open-ended — used once per survey, in Section 6 only (plus an optional "other" free-text field in Section 5 Q17/Q19).** Per the skill's guidance (Group A: 1 question max; Group B: 1–2 questions), open-ended questions are limited to avoid overburdening respondents and to reduce the risk of children inadvertently disclosing identifying or sensitive information in unstructured text. The Section 6 closing question is explicitly optional and framed as a low-pressure invitation rather than a requirement.

## Ethical Considerations

- **No normalisation of bypassing.** Every bypass-related question is phrased observationally ("Have you heard of someone...") rather than participatory ("Have you ever..."/"Did you..."). The standard bypass definition explicitly states "You do not need to have done any of these things to answer the questions," which both clarifies the term and pre-empts any implication that bypassing is expected, common, or condoned. No question is framed in a way that praises, recommends, or provides instructions for any bypass method — methods are named only in the context of "have you heard of this happening," and the survey provides no operational detail beyond what is needed to identify whether the respondent recognises the method.

- **No identifying information about other people.** No question asks the respondent to name a specific friend, sibling, classmate, or any other individual. References to other people are kept categorical ("a friend," "a classmate," "a brother or sister," "someone else"), and the optional free-text fields do not prompt for names.

- **No questions about the respondent's own bypass behaviour.** The survey at no point asks "have you bypassed restrictions" or "have you used [method]" in the first person. All Section 4 and 5 questions are framed strictly in terms of what the respondent has heard about happening to or done by others, consistent with the guardrail that the survey must never elicit a confession of rule-breaking from a minor.

- **Conditional structure and bypass definition placement.** Section 5 is explicitly gated on a "Yes" response to the Section 4 question, so respondents who have not heard of any bypassing are not presented with a list of methods they may not otherwise have considered. The standard bypass definition appears immediately before Section 4 (and therefore before Section 5) in both surveys, ensuring no bypass-related question is asked without first establishing shared, neutral, non-judgmental framing.

- **Voluntariness, anonymity, and low pressure.** Both surveys state at the outset (and at points throughout) that there are no right or wrong answers, that participation in any individual question is optional, and that the respondent can stop at any time. The Group B survey explicitly states responses are anonymous. The facilitator note for Group A instructs adults administering the survey to read questions aloud if needed and to actively reassure children that skipping is acceptable.

- **Tone and emotional safety.** Emoji-based Likert scales, warm framing language ("There are no right or wrong answers here"), and a closing 😊 in the Group A survey are designed to keep the experience light and non-threatening, particularly around the more sensitive bypass-related sections.

## Limitations

- **Self-report bias.** As with all survey instruments, responses reflect what participants are willing and able to report, not necessarily objective behaviour. This is particularly relevant for awareness questions about bypassing, where social desirability or fear of getting someone else "in trouble" could suppress "Yes" responses even when the respondent has relevant knowledge.

- **No measurement of personal bypass behaviour.** By design, the survey cannot and does not measure whether respondents themselves have bypassed age restrictions. This is an intentional ethical trade-off: the survey can describe awareness/exposure to bypassing as a phenomenon among peers, but cannot quantify prevalence of the behaviour itself among respondents.

- **Group A comprehension ceiling.** Even with simplified language and emoji scales, the youngest respondents in Group A (around age 6) may have limited ability to reflect on abstract concepts like "rules making sense" or to reliably recall past events ("have you ever seen..."). Facilitator-read administration can help, but comprehension variance within the 6–12 age band is likely to be substantial, and results should be interpreted with this in mind (e.g., disaggregating by narrower age bands if possible).

- **"Heard of" is an indirect proxy.** Section 4/5 responses measure secondhand awareness (what respondents have heard about others doing), which is an indirect proxy for how widespread bypass methods actually are. It may overstate prevalence (rumour, exaggeration, urban-legend effects among peer groups) or understate it (children may not discuss bypassing openly even if it is common).

- **Selection and consent effects.** Families/schools that opt into a survey about age restrictions and rule-bypassing may not be representative of the broader Roblox-playing population — for example, families with higher engagement in online safety topics may be more likely to participate, potentially skewing awareness levels in either direction.

- **No measurement of severity or context.** A "Yes, I've heard of this" response does not capture how often, how recently, in what context, or with what consequences a bypass method was encountered. The survey is designed for breadth of awareness screening, not depth of incident-level detail.

- **Cross-group comparability is approximate.** While both surveys share a six-section skeleton, the language, scale types (emoji vs. verbal Likert), and item count differ (Group B includes Q8 and Q12, which Group A does not). Comparisons between groups should treat these as parallel-but-not-identical instruments.

## Recommended Administration Notes

- **Setting.** Both surveys are intended for administration in a supervised setting (e.g., at home with a parent/guardian present, or in a school/research setting with a trained facilitator), not for unsupervised online distribution to minors, given the sensitive subject matter.

- **Group A (ages 6–12).**
  - Recommend facilitator-assisted administration: an adult reads each question aloud, in a neutral tone, and records the child's verbal or pointed response (e.g., pointing to a face on the Likert scale).
  - Before starting, the facilitator should reiterate that there are no right or wrong answers, that the child can skip any question, and that they can stop at any time without consequence.
  - For the bypass definition and Section 4/5, facilitators should read the definition slowly and check for understanding (e.g., "Does that make sense? Do you want me to read it again?") before proceeding.
  - Estimated completion time: 5–8 minutes, but may run longer with very young children if read aloud.

- **Group B (ages 13–17).**
  - Can be self-administered (paper or digital form) given the higher reading level, but a facilitator or contact person should be available for questions.
  - Anonymity should be emphasised and genuinely preserved (e.g., no login/account linkage, no collection of names or other directly identifying fields).
  - Estimated completion time: 8–12 minutes.

- **Parental/guardian consent considerations.**
  - Because both surveys involve minors and touch on a sensitive topic (circumvention of platform safety controls), informed parental/guardian consent should be obtained prior to administration for all respondents under the applicable age of consent in the relevant jurisdiction (noting that thresholds vary — see `law-report.md` for jurisdiction-specific minimum ages relevant to children's data and consent).
  - Consent materials should clearly describe: the topic of the survey (Roblox usage and awareness of age rules, including awareness of how others may bypass them), that participation is voluntary, that responses are anonymous/confidential, that the survey does not ask about the child's own rule-breaking, and how data will be stored and used.
  - For Group A, age-appropriate "assent" should also be sought directly from the child in addition to parental consent — i.e., the child should be told in simple terms what the survey is about and asked if they are willing to take part, even if a parent has already consented on their behalf.
  - Researchers/administrators should have a clear protocol for what happens if a child discloses something concerning during the open-ended Section 6 (e.g., a safeguarding referral pathway), even though the survey itself is not designed to elicit such disclosures.
  - Data handling should comply with applicable children's privacy frameworks (e.g., COPPA, UK GDPR/Age Appropriate Design Code, and other regimes identified in `law-report.md`), including data minimisation (no names, no account identifiers, no free-text prompts that invite identifying detail) and secure storage/anonymisation of any optional free-text responses before analysis.
