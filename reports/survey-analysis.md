# Survey Analysis: Roblox Age Restrictions

**Group A** — ages 6–12 (n=16) | **Group B** — ages 13–17 (n=17)

---

## Usage

**The platform's most restricted users are also its most intensive ones — which inverts the expected risk profile.** Group A (ages 6–12) has the most limited accounts yet plays the hardest: 63% play every day, 31% play for more than 2 hours per session, and nearly two-thirds play alongside people they only know online. Group B (ages 13–17) — whose accounts carry fewer restrictions — is far more casual: only 24% play daily, sessions are mostly under an hour, and 82% play exclusively with real-life contacts. Two Group B respondents don't play at all.

This inversion matters. The youngest, most vulnerable group has the deepest platform exposure and the most contact with strangers. The age restriction system is protecting the wrong end of the usage curve.

---

## Awareness of Age Rules

**Children know rules exist but have no meaningful contact with them on the platform — which means they're learning about restrictions through friction and peer gossip, not through education.**

|                                  | Group A (n=16) | Group B (n=17) |
|----------------------------------|:--------------:|:--------------:|
| Knew age rules exist             | 14 (88%)       | 13 (76%)       |
| Knew the specific age limit      | 9 (56%)        | 8 (47%)        |
| Recalled seeing an age warning   | 6 (38%)        | 3 (18%)        |

Awareness drops sharply at each level of specificity. Most children know restrictions exist in principle; far fewer know what they actually are; fewer still have encountered them in the app. In Group B, the most common routes to awareness were online sources (YouTube, social media) and being blocked from a feature. Not one Group B respondent said a parent told them. Roblox's in-app communication about its own rules is reaching fewer than one in five older users (18%).

Counterintuitively, Group A shows higher rule awareness than Group B across all three measures. This likely reflects that the restrictions land harder on younger accounts — they encounter the walls more often — rather than any difference in how the platform communicates its policies.

---

## Perceptions of Safety and Enforcement

**Both groups doubt the rules work, but for different reasons: younger children experience them as arbitrary barriers; older children understand the rationale but have lost confidence in the execution.**

On whether age restrictions effectively protect younger users, Group A and Group B split similarly — roughly 38–41% positive, 29–38% negative across both groups. But the "make sense" question reveals a sharp divergence. Only 31% of Group A respondents thought the rules made sense, with 56% saying they didn't. Group B went the opposite way: 59% agreed the restrictions make sense given the platform's content, with only 12% disagreeing.

This gap is not a contradiction — it reflects different vantage points. Younger children feel the restrictions as communication blocks (they can't chat with friends outside their age bracket) without understanding the protective intent. Older children, who read the rationale more clearly, accept the principle but reject the implementation: 47% of Group B said Roblox doesn't do enough to enforce its rules, against only 24% who agreed it does.

**The content exposure data is the starkest finding in this section.** Nearly half of Group A respondents (44%) said they had encountered something on Roblox they felt too young to see. This is the group with the most restricted accounts. Whatever filtering is in place is failing the children most at risk from inappropriate content. Group B reported lower exposure (24%), which is consistent with their more limited and socially contained play patterns.

---

## Awareness of Bypass Methods

**Circumvention of age restrictions is not a marginal behaviour — it is openly discussed and widely known across both age groups, with the youngest children having the highest awareness of the most fundamental bypass.**

More than half of each group had heard of someone bypassing Roblox's age restrictions (Group A: 56%, Group B: 65%). Awareness of specific methods:

| Method                              | Group A (n=15 answering) | Group B (n=17) |
|-------------------------------------|:------------------------:|:--------------:|
| False date of birth when signing up | 14 (93%)                 | 12 (71%)       |
| Creating multiple accounts          | 9 (60%)                  | 12 (71%)       |
| Using unofficial/modified Roblox    | 7 (47%)                  | 5 (29%)        |
| Using a parent's or sibling's account | 8 (53%)                | 8 (47%)        |
| VPN or region-spoofing tool         | 4 (27%)                  | 8 (47%)        |

The false date of birth finding is critical. Entering a false DOB bypasses the account creation system entirely — it is the first line of age enforcement — and 93% of Group A respondents who answered this question had heard of it. This is higher than the corresponding figure for Group B (71%). The children the system is most designed to protect are the ones most aware of how to defeat it at the point of entry.

Multiple-account creation follows a similar pattern: 60% in Group A and 71% in Group B are aware this is used to evade restrictions or bans. Together, false DOB and multiple accounts represent the two lowest-effort bypasses, and they are the most commonly known.

VPN awareness is higher in Group B (47% vs 27%), consistent with greater technical familiarity among older users. Unofficial or modified Roblox clients show the opposite pattern — more familiar to Group A (47% vs 29%), possibly reflecting greater willingness to explore non-official routes among very young players who encounter restrictions they don't understand.

One Group A respondent described a bypass method not covered by the survey: using a photograph of an older person to defeat Roblox's facial age verification. This is a targeted, deliberate attack on a specific security measure, and it is being discussed among children aged 6–12.

---

## Open-Ended Comments

**The volume and tone of open-ended responses reveal who is bearing the cost of the current system.** Group A produced seven times the substantive commentary of Group B (44% of respondents vs one). Younger children used the space to express frustration and scepticism, not compliance:

- The communication restrictions are the primary irritant: children are blocked from talking with friends outside their age bracket and see this as the rules' main effect, not its safety rationale
- Several respondents independently flagged that bypass routes are obvious and social ("you can easily bypass them by telling an older person"), suggesting restriction evasion is actively discussed and normalised in this age group
- One respondent identified cyberbullying and racism as bigger concerns than age restrictions — suggesting children have a lived threat model that doesn't align with the platform's restriction framework

Group B's near-silence (one substantive response, describing how the respondent avoids online strangers by communicating through separate apps) suggests older users have adapted their behaviour pragmatically rather than engaging critically with the rules.

---

## Summary

The data points to a system that has failed at its primary objective. The children most at risk — the youngest, most intensive users with the most online contact — report the highest rates of inappropriate content exposure, the highest awareness of bypass methods, and the strongest scepticism that the rules protect them. Meanwhile, the system's visible friction (communication blocks, feature locks) is generating resentment without generating safety.

Four findings stand out:

1. **Content filters are failing where they matter most.** Nearly half of Group A saw content they felt too young for, despite having the most restricted accounts.
2. **The fundamental bypass — false date of birth — is nearly universally known among 6–12 year olds (93%).** If the account creation system can be defeated this easily, every downstream restriction rests on a broken foundation.
3. **Enforcement credibility has collapsed among older users.** Group B accepts the principle of age restrictions but 47% doubt Roblox enforces them. Accepting a rule and expecting it to be applied are different things.
4. **Parents are not in the loop.** No Group B respondent learned about age restrictions from a parent. The safety system depends entirely on Roblox's own communication — which is reaching very few users through the platform itself.
